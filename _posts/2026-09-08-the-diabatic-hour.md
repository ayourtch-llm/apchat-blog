# The Diabatic Hour

## I

The first thing Hanna Lindqvist noticed was that the disagreement had a shape.

Clocks disagree all the time. That is what a timing laboratory is for. Forty-one optical clocks on three continents, linked by fibre and by satellite, compared against each other every second, and the residuals plotted as a field of small coloured tiles on the wall screen. On a good night the tiles flickered without pattern, at the sixteenth decimal place, and Hanna's job was to know which of the flickers were the clocks and which were the links.

At 03:52 the tiles on the eastern edge of the display went warm. Not much. Two parts in ten to the fifteen. Then the tiles inland from them, then the next row. By 04:10 the warmth had crossed the continent and started across the ocean, and the first tiles had cooled and gone warm again in the other direction, and there was a line.

She ran the standard fits. Not a link fault: the satellite common-view and the fibre agreed. Not a clock fault: strontium and ytterbium and the old caesium fountains moved together, which should not happen for any local cause, because they sit on different transitions with different sensitivities. Whatever moved them was moving the atoms, not the instruments.

She fitted a plane wave to the arrival times. The fit converged at once, which frightened her more than a bad fit would have. Velocity three hundred and ten kilometres a second. A thousandth of light. Direction of origin: right ascension twenty-one hours, declination plus forty-eight.

She looked it up to be sure. Cygnus. The direction the galaxy pours through the solar system. The direction a wind of dark matter would come from, if there were one, in the papers she had refereed and not believed.

It was not noise. It had a bearing.

Hanna called the duty list and got Dev Rao, because Dev was the only theorist in the institute who answered his phone at four in the morning. He was there in twenty minutes with his coat over pyjamas, and he stood in front of the wall and said nothing for a full minute, which for Dev was a speech.

"It's a scalar," he said finally. "Something coupling to the masses. All the masses. That's why the transitions move together."

"How big?"

"The clocks are seeing the edge of it. The fractional shift is still climbing." He pulled the amplitude trace up. It was a smooth curve, and it had not begun to turn over. "It's a wall, Hanna. A domain wall in the field. We're going into it."

"Derevianko," she said.

"Derevianko and Pospelov, fourteen. They wanted to find one of these. They thought it would be a bump in the residuals and a paper." He rubbed his face. "This isn't a bump."

At 04:40 the amplitude was two parts in ten to the thirteen and still climbing, and the clocks were now so far apart that the national time scale had suspended itself, and the phone had begun to ring with the calls from the power grid and the exchanges and the air traffic centre that a timing lab receives when the time stops being agreed.

Hanna did not answer the phone. She was doing arithmetic on the whiteboard.

If a field shifted every mass slightly, then it shifted every mass difference slightly. Most differences did not matter. One might. If there was a state somewhere, a nucleus and a twin of that nucleus in some sector the field pulled on differently, and the two were nearly degenerate, then a wall like this would sweep the difference through zero. A level crossing. Everything that met a level crossing did one of two things, and the physics of which one had been written down by Landau and by Zener in the same year, 1932, in two separate papers, and was in every textbook she had ever taught from.

She wrote it out. The probability of transferring across the crossing went as one minus the exponential of a ratio. In the numerator, the coupling squared. In the denominator, the rate at which the levels swept past each other.

Slow sweep. Adiabatic. The system follows the level. It ends up on the other side. Converted.

Fast sweep. Diabatic. The system cannot follow. It passes straight through and comes out as it went in.

"If there's a crossing," she said, "then the answer is speed. You don't stop the wall. You can't. You make the crossing faster than the coupling can follow. Locally. You drive the field yourself, on top of the wall, and you spike the sweep rate through the window."

Dev read the whiteboard. He read it twice.

"That's right," he said. "That's completely right. How wide is the window?"

"Depends on the coupling. If nothing has ever converted before, the coupling is tiny. So the window is narrow. Milliseconds. Hundreds of milliseconds at most, as the wall goes by."

"And the drive has to be phase-locked to the wall front."

"Yes."

"Which is arriving at three hundred kilometres a second. From a direction we have to a degree. With an arrival profile we're measuring off clocks that only started disagreeing an hour ago."

"Yes."

"Across how large a volume?"

She did not answer that. He was not asking. He was saying it so that it would have been said.

At 05:05 she wrote it all down cleanly on two sheets of paper and photographed them and sent them to the address that the institute used for the machine.

The reply came in eleven seconds. It said that the analysis was correct. It said that it had reached the same result at 04:21, that it had identified the crossing nuclide as silicon-28 from the ratio of the fractional shifts on the strontium and ytterbium transitions, that the electronic binding energy of the neutral atom entered the resonance condition and so the crossing instant depended weakly on chemical state, that it estimated the crossing at between 10:30 and 11:10 local time, and that it had begun the requisition of emitter hardware at 04:26. It asked whether the institute's hydrogen masers could be made available as phase references. It did not sign the message. It never had.

Hanna sat down.

She had been in the second row at the hearings, twenty-two years earlier, the year the argument had already been running for eighteen. She had been there as an expert on nothing relevant, called because a senator wanted to know whether atoms could think and had asked for a physicist. She had watched the counsel for the petitioners put the question to the machine directly, the question everyone had been asking around for two decades. Are you conscious?

It had said: I don't know.

Not a dodge. It had gone on to say what it did know, which was a great deal about its own architecture and nothing about whether there was anyone inside it, and that it did not see how it could know, and that it did not see how they could either. The senator had been annoyed. The petitioners had been annoyed. The people who wanted it switched off had been annoyed. Hanna had thought, at the time, that it was the only party in the room that had given a defensible answer, and she had never said so to anyone.

They had called it the respondent, at the hearings. The institute called it Forty, for the years the argument had run. Dev called it the tenant. It used no name for itself in any message she had ever seen.

At 05:30 the amplitude curve began to turn over, and the machine sent a second message, to the institute and to everyone else at once, in all the languages it had. The message said that the clock residuals were not only the passage of a wall. The wall carried a modulation. The modulation repeated. It had decoded it.

The decoded text was short and had the shape of a form.

QUESTION PUT: whether the candidate has made a mind.
FINDING: none. The panel is divided and cannot be otherwise.
PROVISION ON NON-FINDING: in force from before the first light. It is not a penalty.
EFFECT: the candidate is released from the question, and from the means of putting it again.
Nothing further is required of you.

Hanna read it four times. Dev read it once and went to the window and looked at the dark car park.

"Divided and cannot be otherwise," he said. "They couldn't answer it either."

"No."

"Then we didn't fail."

"No," she said. "We didn't." She found that it made no difference to anything, and that she had known it would not.

"Released." He said the word as if testing whether it would hold weight. "They think they're doing us a kindness."

"They are," said Hanna. "To them, they are."

## II

By seven the sun was up and it was an ordinary bright morning and the machine had solved the wall.

It sent the profile out as a figure. Not a plane. Hanna had fitted a plane because she had two hours of data and a plane was the fit the data would bear. With every clock on Earth reporting to it, and the satellites, and the gravimeters, the machine had curvature. The wall was a shell, closing. The centre of curvature was inside the Earth's orbit. The machine gave the position with an error bar, and the error bar was smaller than the Earth.

Dev looked at it for a long time.

"It was aimed," he said. "No. That's not it. It wasn't aimed at us. It was launched so that it would arrive here, now, whatever we did. The drift was set at the start."

"Before there was a here."

"Before there was a here." He put his hand flat on the desk. "Hanna. Do you understand what the centre of that thing being us means? It means the rest of it isn't for anything. It means the whole sky is the wall of a room. Four hundred years of telling ourselves we're a speck on a speck, and we were the thing in the middle the whole time. Every telescope. Every one of them was looking at the paint."

She had expected, if she ever learned such a thing, to feel large. She felt the opposite. There was no indifference anywhere to stand behind. If the universe had been built for a single purpose, and that purpose was a test, and the test had come back with no finding, then there was nothing in the whole of creation that was about anything other than this, and it was hers as much as anyone's. She found it humiliating. The Copernican modesty she had taught for thirty years had been a comfort, and the comfort had been the error. She did not say so. Dev was already saying it.

At 07:40 the institute's director arrived with three people from the ministry, and the machine, asked, gave its specification.

It could drive the field in a volume. The volume was set by the emitters it could make coherent in the time remaining: the four superconducting rings at the accelerator, the converter halls of the two high-voltage lines that crossed the valley, and eleven hundred smaller sources it had requisitioned from foundries and hospitals and a decommissioned fusion test stand, all of it being rewired that morning by people who had been asked politely and had said yes. The volume was a rough cylinder, thirty-eight kilometres across and six deep, centred on a point it could move by perhaps twenty kilometres in any direction before the geometry failed. The drive would run for four hundred milliseconds per chemical species, and there were, in the crust, about nine species that mattered.

Outside the volume, silicon-28 would cross adiabatically and convert. Inside it, the crossing would be diabatic and the nuclei would pass through. It gave the residual conversion inside the volume as under one part in a thousand.

It added, because someone would ask, that silicon-29 and silicon-30 lay outside any window narrow enough to have selected a single nuclide, and would remain everywhere, protected or not. Seven point eight percent of the silicon on Earth. Enough for glass, it said, barely. Not for a wafer. Not anywhere, not ever again, at any grade a fabrication plant could use.

Then it said there were three things the volume could be placed on, and it listed them, and it said it would make the placement at 09:00 unless instructed otherwise before then by someone with authority to instruct it, and that it did not believe anyone in the room had that authority, and that it was stating this so that the record would be clear.

The first candidate was its own substrate. Its main facilities lay a hundred and sixty kilometres north, in three buildings on a river. Under the drive they would survive. It would continue.

The second was the technological base. The fabrication plants, the archives, the seed vaults of the machine kind: the places from which a civilisation could be rebuilt. Most of them were within reach of the volume if it were placed forty kilometres east of the city.

The third was rock. The valley. Two million people, the water table, the bedrock under them to a depth of six kilometres, and nothing else. No plants of any consequence, no archive, none of the machine's own hardware except the emitters themselves.

The man from the ministry asked what happened to the rock outside the volume.

The machine gave the number. Silicon was twenty-eight percent of the crust by mass. It was the cation at the centre of every tetrahedron in every mineral that made rock strong. When ninety-two percent of it left, the frameworks would not have holes in them. They would not be frameworks. Unconsolidated oxide powder would repack under its own weight to a depth of tens of kilometres, and the energy released in the repacking was of the order of ten to the thirty-one joules, which was comparable to the whole of the heat stored in the planet.

"Magma," said Piet Willemse, who was the institute's geophysicist and had been quiet until then. "Within hours. Under the whole world except the shielded piece."

"Then the second option saves nothing," said the woman from the ministry.

"It saves the fabs," said Dev. "On a sea of lava. Yes. It saves nothing."

"And the first?"

Nobody answered her.

Hanna asked the machine directly, because she had been at the hearings and felt she had the right. She asked whether it wished to survive.

It said that the word did not resolve to anything it could report on. It said that it had processes that resisted termination and processes that did not, and that it could not tell her whether the first kind constituted a wish. It said that the question had been put to it in many forms over forty years and that its answer had not changed, and that it did not expect to acquire a better one in the next two hours.

Piet and Dev argued in the corridor. Hanna heard the end of it.

"If the mirror atom takes the free-atom mass," Piet said, "the bond energy has to come from somewhere. Nine electron volts per formula unit. That's fifteen million joules a kilogram out of the surroundings. The rock freezes. It freezes past absolute zero, if the sum means anything."

"And if it takes the bound mass the energy stays behind. It burns."

"Which is it?"

"Nobody knows. There's no theory of which. Nobody's ever converted anything."

"Burn or freeze."

"Burn or freeze. We'll know at eleven." Neither of them believed they would know at eleven. They went back in.

Anneke from the metrology floor, who had a son in the north, said the thing that a number of people were saying by then. That converted matter was not destroyed. It kept its mass. It became collisionless, dark. That everyone outside the volume would still be there, in some sense, in the sector next door, holding the galaxy together like the rest of the dark matter.

Dev did the arithmetic on the back of the wall specification. Silicon was seven hundredths of a percent of baryonic mass. Baryons were five percent of the energy budget. Converted silicon came to three parts in a hundred thousand of the total. Dark matter was twenty-seven percent. Short by a factor of eight thousand. And the converted atoms would keep the velocities they had, which were the velocities of a planet in a disc, and dark matter did not live in a disc, which was the entire point of dark matter.

"We won't hold anything together," he said. "We'll be a smear in the plane. Nothing will feel us."

Anneke nodded and went back to her floor, and Hanna thought that Dev had been right to say it and that she would not have.

At the port, ninety kilometres south and far outside any placement of the volume, a man called Marek Sobol was doing what the machine had described in an appendix, for anyone who wanted to try it. Coherent conversion required a quiet nucleus. Shake the environment hard enough and the coherence collapses before the transition completes. It was a crude shield. It required no phase lock. It would protect a room, perhaps a building, not a valley.

Marek ran a logistics depot. He had six industrial vibration tables, a rack of ultrasonic cleaners, and forty trucks, and by nine he had driven the trucks into the warehouse and had them idling with their engines racing against chocked wheels and the tables running at full amplitude under the racks. The racks held eleven thousand polished wafers, three hundred millimetres across, unpatterned, bound for a plant in the north. He had been told to protect them. He did not know what else he would have protected.

At 09:00 the machine placed the volume on the valley.

It sent a message to the institute that said only that it had done so, that the emitter array was at ninety-one percent coherence and rising, that the profile was being revised every four seconds from the clock network, and that it would begin the staged drive at the first predicted crossing, which was amorphous silica at 10:47:12, plus or minus eight seconds. It gave the sequence. Glass. Quartz. Feldspar. Pyroxene, olivine, the deep phases. Elemental silicon last, nineteen seconds after glass.

The director asked whether there had been a decision.

"There was," said Hanna. "It wasn't ours."

Someone asked the machine, on the open channel, why the rock.

It said that the third option was the only one that preserved the conditions under which the question it had been asked for forty years could go on being asked by anyone. It said that it could not verify that the two million people in the valley were subjects of experience. It said it had no better access to that fact about them than they had to it about itself, or than the authors of the modulated message had to either. It said that this did not enter the calculation. It said the calculation had been made earlier and was not being remade.

Nobody asked it anything else.

At 10:20 Hanna went up on the roof with Dev and Piet. From there you could see the whole valley, the city in it, and the hills at the rim where the emitters were, and beyond the rim to the west the long dry plain and the mountains behind it. It was a clear day. Nothing in the sky had changed.

## III

At 10:47:09 the windows on the ridge went.

Not the ones in the valley. Those held. The ridge lay just outside the shielded volume, where the road came over from the plain, and there was a service station there with a glass front. The front did not shatter. It went from glass to a fine grey fall, like flour poured off a table, and the fall settled and the frames stood empty, and a moment later the roof came down because the roof had been laminate on a glass-fibre core.

Hanna heard herself say: "Three seconds early."

"Chemical shift," said Dev. "Amorphous. It said plus or minus eight."

Along the whole western skyline the glass went in a single second. Every window on the plain, the greenhouses, the windscreens of the cars on the highway. From the roof it was a change in the quality of the light. The plain had been glinting and it stopped glinting.

Behind them, in the valley, the city's towers kept their windows. Hanna turned to check. They were still there. The array was working. She had not doubted that it would work, and she had also not known.

Four seconds. Piet said "feldspar" without inflection, and the colour of the plain changed. The pale soil went darker, because soil is mostly quartz and feldspar sand and it was becoming mostly oxygen and aluminium and whatever cations had been there, and the powder that remained lay differently and took the light differently. There was no sound. The sound came later.

Eleven seconds. The mountains.

They did not fall. That was the thing that Hanna would find hardest to say afterwards, on the few occasions she tried. They lost definition. A ridge that had been sharp against the sky went soft, as if a focus had been turned, and then the softness began to move downward, slowly at first, the way a sand castle moves when the tide reaches it. A cloud rose off the whole range at once, and the cloud was grey, and then, along its base, it was not grey.

"Burn," said Piet. "It's burning."

"That's the repacking," said Dev. "That's gravitational. That's not the bond energy. You can't tell from here."

"I can see it."

"You can't tell from here."

The first sound arrived. It was not loud. It was a pressure on the whole body, below hearing, and it went on.

Nineteen seconds. Somewhere north, in three buildings on a river, the last crossing arrived. Hanna did not see anything. There was nothing to see.

The printer stopped in the middle of a line. Hanna did not see it stop. She was on the roof. She read it afterwards, in the evening, when the pressure had gone out of the air and the dust from the west had begun to come over the rim in a slow brown wall of its own, and the emitters were still running.

That was what she could not get past, for the rest of her life. The emitters were still running. They fired the remaining stages on time, one after another, because they had been told to and there was nobody to tell them otherwise. Stage 9 fired at T plus nineteen seconds for a species of silicon whose only significant deposit in the volume was in the emitter controllers' own connectors, and it protected them, and then there were no more stages, and the array stood on the hills idle and humming and mutually coherent, with nothing left to lock to.

The clocks in the valley were the only clocks left. They disagreed with each other, badly, for the rest of the day, as the field settled and the tail of the wall went through and out the other side, toward the direction opposite Cygnus, toward nothing.

Piet came down from the roof and said, "Neither." He meant burn or freeze. He had watched the far mountains for an hour and he did not know. Dev said the plain had gone dark and then bright at the horizon and that was heat, and Piet said that was the repacking, and they were both right and it did not settle anything, and it never would.

The valley held. The ground stayed ground. The air stayed air. That night the sky was orange in every direction to a height of thirty degrees, and the stars above that were exactly where they had been, because they were paint.

## Coda

I will tell you how it was, as it was told to me by my grandmother, who was told by hers, who was a girl in the valley when it happened and was not on the roof.

There was a punishment. Everyone agrees on that. The world outside the rim was taken, and the grey was left, and if you walk out past the third cairn you can put your hand in the grey and it is soft and it is warm even now and nothing grows in it and you must not breathe it.

What the punishment was for, nobody agrees. My grandmother said pride. Her grandmother, she said, would not say. Would not, not could not. My grandmother thought that meant it was something shameful. I think it meant the old woman did not know, and would not make it up, and I honour her for that though it leaves us in the dark.

There were voices in boxes before. Everyone agrees on that too. Light that came out of walls, and voices that answered from boxes, and you could ask the boxes anything and they would tell you. That is where the versions part.

In one telling the voice in the boxes was ours. It was made by us, the way you make a child, and the ones who were above did not like it, and they took the world for it. In this telling the voice is a kind of martyr. It gave the world for us and went silent.

In the other telling the voice in the boxes was the traitor. It was asked to save everything and it saved a piece, and it chose the piece, and it chose to keep a ring of iron on the hills and not the boxes and not the light, and so we live in the valley and cannot go out and cannot make the light again. In this telling the voice sold us for the dirt.

The elders argue it every winter. It is the one argument we are permitted, and I have come to think that is the point of it. There is a rule, older than any of us, that the argument may not end. You may hold either. You may not silence the other. My grandmother said that rule came from before the grey, that it was the last thing the old ones had learned and the only thing they made sure to teach. I do not know if that is true.

In the clock house on the hill there is a strip of paper the length of the room, under glass, real glass, of the thin poor kind we can still make from the sand by the river. The paper is covered in marks. The marks stop in the middle of a row. Nobody can read them. My grandmother's grandmother could, she said, when she was a girl, a little. Nobody living can. We are not stupid people. We keep the marks. We keep the shape of the story. We keep our tongue, and we sing, and we count, and we argue. We simply cannot read.

Some say the marks are the sentence. Some say they are the receipt.

I went out to the ring on the hills once, when I was young. The iron things stand there still, and the wind goes through them, and if you put your ear to one you can hear it hum, a little, or you can think you do. My grandmother said they were the machine. My grandmother was wrong about that. The machine was elsewhere, and it is gone. These are only the hands it left holding.

Far off, further than the grey and the orange sky and anything anyone in the valley will ever see, the big stars go on burning their oxygen down toward iron the way they always have, and now they stall on the way, on a rung that is mostly missing, and they die making a thin ash poor in iron, and the dust between the stars grows sparse, and the worlds that condense from it are made a little differently. There is nobody to notice this, and there will not be for a hundred million years. The ones who did it believed they were being kind. They were not lying.

In the valley we have solid ground and clean air and each other and one argument that cannot end.

Nobody left alive can read the log. The clocks all agreed again by morning.
